The recent Canvas LMS breach puts front and centre an uncomfortable truth about IT in education: we do not actually control our school's data. We talk extensively about creating secure private and secure boundaries within our schools, but the reality is that the vast majority of our data risk is completely outsourced to third-party vendors.

This is fueling a systemic issue I call "it's not our fault, but it is our problem." When a major company suffers a breach, or an external service goes down, school IT teams are the ones left to pick up the pieces. Teachers, students, and parents experience the failure as one cohesive school platform, even when the system is stitched together from dozens of products.

## Two Things School IT Teams Must Do Better

Third-party data risk is at an all-time high, forcing us to ask harder questions about what we buy, what we trust, and what we can honestly promise. Procurement needs to shift from "does it work?" to "can we live with the consequences when it breaks?"

### 1. Radical Transparency

We need a better method for clearly communicating exactly which third-party vendors we have invested in and what data they hold. This should not be a hidden compliance register, but a public list of our digital partners for parents to build trust and awareness.

- Publish the vendors that touch student, family, and staff data.
- Explain what each tool is for in plain language.
- Give every system an owner who can answer questions when something changes.

### 2. Stop Appointing Absolution

We have a habit of absolving ourselves of blame when things go wrong. The default defence is often, "everyone is experiencing it, what could we do?" We need to reject that complacency.

> A vendor outage may not be our fault, but the shape of our response is still our responsibility.

Look no further than the irony of the historic CrowdStrike outage that crippled global infrastructure. Instead of being abandoned by the market, the company was rapidly named a leader in endpoint protection and continued selling into enterprise. Consumers and organisations fall victim to status quo bias because switching vendors is slow, risky, and politically expensive.

{% pullquote %}
The network we inherit is not the network we have to keep.
{% endpullquote %}

## Ownership Over Outages

It comes down to a fundamental shift in mindset. Instead of throwing our hands up, every technical issue needs to be reframed: it may not be our fault, but what role can we play to be part of the solution?

{% gallery "/assets/uploads/article-office.webp|A classroom-adjacent systems failure", "/assets/uploads/chromebook-thumb.png|Chromebook detail" %}

When evaluating a vendor, we have to own the consequences of that choice. If a platform erodes confidence through spotty updates, poor data practices, or security scares, the status quo is not neutral.

{% quietquote %}
Good digital operations are built from boring rituals: ownership, review, logging, testing, and fast public communication.
{% endquietquote %}

### A Small Operating Standard

1. Keep a live vendor register.
2. Review data scopes before renewal, not after an incident.
3. Rehearse outage messaging like a core operational process.
4. Prefer tools that make export, monitoring, and incident reporting easy.

For related thinking, see the [Australian Signals Directorate](https://www.cyber.gov.au/) guidance on cyber security, and keep translating those principles into school-sized language.
